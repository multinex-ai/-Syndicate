# Narration

Narration is heard once, at the narrator's pace. Write for the ear, and for Kokoro specifically.

## Sentences
- One idea per sentence. Most sentences 8 to 20 words. Follow two long ones with a short one.
- Put the new information at the end of the sentence, where the voice lands.
- Prefer concrete nouns and active verbs. "The river shrank by half" beats "A significant reduction in volume occurred."
- Let the picture do the describing. Narration says what the picture cannot: when, why, how much, so what. Never narrate what is plainly on screen ("Here we see a rocket").
- Leave silence. End a chapter on a short sentence; the pad after narration gives the picture room.

## For the synthetic voice
- Spell numbers the way they should be spoken when it matters: "twenty twenty-four", "three point two million". Kokoro may read "2024" or "3.2M" oddly.
- Expand abbreviations on first use and avoid them after: "the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter", then "the orbiter".
- Write names phonetically only if the voice gets them wrong in a test; otherwise use the real spelling so the captions are correct.
- No markup, brackets, stage directions, emoji or headings in `narration`. The voice reads everything aloud. The tools reject brackets and "(pause)".
- Avoid strings of sibilants and tongue-twisters; read every chapter aloud once.
- English narration uses `bm_george` at 0.94. Spanish uses `em_alex`. Write Spanish narration natively, not as a literal translation.

## Claims in narration
- Match the strength of the words to the strength of the evidence:
  - documented: "records show", "according to the agency's report"
  - attributed: "investigators said", "the company claimed"
  - inferred: "this suggests", "one likely reason"
  - unknown: "it is still not clear whether"
- Attribute disputed claims to who made them, in the sentence.
- Dates and numbers come from a source. Round only when the source supports the rounded figure.
- Hypothetical scenes are announced as hypothetical in the narration itself ("Imagine..." "This is a reconstruction, not footage of the event.").

## Openings that work
- A precise image and a gap: "On the morning of June fifteenth, the surface of the Moon dropped two hundred degrees in under an hour. Nobody was there to feel it."
- A reversal: "For thirty years, the fix worked. Then it became the problem."
- A question the viewer has had: "You have probably wondered why..."

## Endings that work
- Return to the opening image with new meaning.
- A single consequence, stated plainly.
- A question handed to the viewer, if the film has earned it.
Avoid closing with a summary list, a slogan, or a call to action the evidence does not support.
